Type

The mineral families, hardness and colors behind every gemstone.

Every gemstone begins as a mineral. Its chemistry decides its color, its hardness decides how it lives on the hand, and together they define its character.

Six Signature Stones

A first introduction to the families you will meet most often when choosing a fine colored gemstone.

Ruby

Corundum • Mohs 9

The most coveted red. Its glow comes from chromium, and the finest "pigeon's blood" shades are among the rarest and most valuable colored stones in the world.

Sapphire

Corundum • Mohs 9

Best known in deep, velvety blue, sapphire in fact occurs in nearly every color. Exceptional hardness makes it ideal for rings worn every day.

Emerald

Beryl • Mohs 7.5–8

Prized for its saturated green and its delicate internal "jardin". Emeralds are cherished for character, and are best given gentle care.

Amethyst

Quartz • Mohs 7

A regal violet that ranges from soft lilac to deep royal purple. Generous in size and rich in color, it is a favourite for statement jewels.

Tourmaline

Tourmaline • Mohs 7–7.5

The widest palette in the gem kingdom: pink, green, blue and even two colors in one crystal. Lively, versatile and endlessly collectible.

Topaz

Topaz • Mohs 8

From warm golden sherry to sky blue, topaz offers clarity and brilliance with very good hardness. Its rarest shade is natural Imperial orange-pink.

Understanding Hardness

The Mohs scale ranks a mineral's resistance to scratching from 1 to 10. For rings and bracelets worn daily, we recommend stones of 7 or above. Softer gems are exquisite in earrings and pendants, where they are less exposed.

Species and Variety

A species is the mineral itself, such as corundum. A variety is its colored expression: ruby is red corundum, sapphire is every other color. Knowing the difference helps you read a gemstone report and compare stones fairly.

Hardness measures scratch resistance, not toughness. A stone can be hard yet still chip on a sharp blow.
